During Planning Poker discussions, teams sometimes face challenges in reaching a common estimate. In such situations, it may seem convenient to assign separate estimates for different roles instead of agreeing on one overall value.

For instance, if developers and testers view the effort differently, someone might suggest estimating "development points" and "testing points" independently for the same backlog item.

While this approach may appear helpful at first, it often creates more complexity than value.

Story points are intended to represent the total effort required to complete a user story from start to finish. This includes every activity and skill needed to deliver a fully functional feature.

A developer might think, "The coding effort feels like five points, while testing may require about three more, so the overall effort is eight points." Although this example focuses on two roles, the same principle applies when additional disciplines are involved.

A common concern is whether one team member can accurately judge the effort required from another specialization. Initially, this may be difficult, particularly when a team is newly formed or lacks experience working together.

However, estimation is based on relative sizing rather than exact calculations. Over time, team members develop a better understanding of how different activities contribute to overall effort. As collaboration improves, the team becomes capable of assigning a single estimate that reflects the complete scope of work.

The question then becomes: what benefit is gained by maintaining separate estimates for each role?

If dividing estimates by skill area consistently led to better planning, forecasting, or decision-making, there could be a strong argument for doing so. For example, knowing that a story contains five points of development work and three points of testing effort would be useful if it influenced how the team organized delivery.

In practice, however, separate estimates rarely change team behavior. Whether a story is represented as 5 + 3 or simply as 8, the overall planning outcome is usually the same. The added complexity often introduces unintended consequences, such as tracking individual-role velocity instead of focusing on the team's collective delivery capability.

Some organizations, especially those involving highly specialized disciplines such as game development, have experimented with role-based estimates. In environments where programming, art, audio, and quality assurance efforts differ significantly, separate sizing may initially seem justified.

Yet many teams eventually conclude that the additional effort required to create, maintain, and report multiple estimates does not provide sufficient value. As a result, they often transition back to a single estimate that represents the entire work item.

Using one consolidated story point estimate for each backlog item encourages a team-oriented mindset, simplifies planning, and aligns with Agile principles by focusing on the successful delivery of completed functionality rather than individual contributions.
